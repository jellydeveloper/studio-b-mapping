Mapping Access at Home

I hurt my back right before our campus mapping workshop. The 15-minute walk to RPI became the barrier, so I mapped the one place I could still move through: the shared house I live in. This site holds both assignments: HW2 (map, flows, photos, reflection, field notes) and HW3 (a modified map of three items, design responses, and a written response).

Reflection

The space and the change of plans

For HW1 I planned to map three campus buildings: Folsom Library, West Hall, and the Rensselaer Union. A few days before the mapping workshop I hurt my back, and walking to campus stopped being something I could do without pain. My house is more than a 15-minute walk from RPI, and that walk is now the barrier. So I could not join my group, and I mapped the one space I could actually move through: the shared house I live in. It has a ground floor, a basement, and a second floor with numbered rooms (my room is #11). I did this assignment alone.

The change ended up being part of the research. The question I started with in HW1 was about the gap between what a space looks like on paper and what it feels like to use when something is "temporarily out of order." This time the thing out of order was my own body, and I got to see that gap from the inside.

What I started with

I carried over the guiding questions from my HW1 field notes and used them as my criteria: Can you find the entrance and know you are in the right place without asking? How does moving between floors work, and is there any alternative to stairs? Where are restrooms, and are they shared or private? Who is the space designed around, and who has to work around it? What unwritten rules does the space communicate?

My main concern was simple: with an injured back, which parts of my everyday route through the house cost me effort or pain that I never noticed before? I walked every route I normally take (front door to my room, room to the shared restroom, ground floor to basement), took photos, and wrote down what my body had to do at each point: push, pull, lift, grip, wait, or turn around.

Standards and evidence

I used three kinds of evidence. First, physical features I could count or describe: number of stairways, handrails, restrooms, and doors with closers. Second, my own body as a measuring tool: whether something hurt, how many times I had to stop, and whether I could do it while carrying something. Third, information and signage: whether the building tells you its address, its rules, or its routes. I rated the house as "medium." There is no step-free way between floors, but every staircase has handrails on both sides, which made the stairs possible for me even on bad days.

What I found

The front door was the biggest problem. It has an overhead door closer, a keypad lock, and a handwritten sign that says "Please lock & shut door nice + tight." The closer makes the door heavy, and pushing it with a hurt back is the hardest single movement in my day. The sign is an unwritten rule becoming a written one: the house expects everyone to pull the door shut firmly, which assumes a body that can do that.

The address is not visible from the front door at all. The only place it is written is on a piece of paper at the back door, where the mail comes in. A delivery driver, a visitor, or an ambulance crew coming to the front would not be able to confirm they are at the right house. The building is legible to the mail carrier but not to a guest.

There are three sets of stairs: one to the right of the front entrance going up to the second floor, one next to it going down to the basement, and another straight back and to the right that also goes up. The treads are worn and the paint is peeling in places. At one landing, a gap in the railing is patched with plywood, which tells me repairs here happen as quick fixes rather than planned updates. There is a metal chair at the top of the stairs. It may not have been put there for this reason, but it works as a place to rest after climbing.

Restrooms are unevenly spread. There is one shared all-gender restroom downstairs, between rooms 2 and 3, for all six ground floor rooms. Upstairs, rooms 7, 8, and 11 (mine) each have a private bathroom, and there is one more between rooms 9 and 10. Which room you rent decides whether you have to use the stairs or a hallway to reach a toilet. I happen to have a private bathroom, which made my injury much easier to live with.

Outside, the house is in a dark area. A motion sensor light turns on over the front door at night, which helps at the door itself, but the street around the house is dark and I feel unsafe walking alone at night. Combined with the distance to campus, my access to RPI depends on the time of day, my back, and whether I have company.

Surprises and frustrations

What surprised me most was how much the door mattered compared to the stairs. I expected the stairs to be the barrier, but the handrails made them manageable, while the door has nothing to make it easier. The feature I pass every day and never noticed was the door closer itself. The accessibility feature not meant for me that I use anyway is the second handrail; I always used one side, and now I use both. The frustration was missing the group workshop, which showed me how quickly access to a class activity disappears when getting to campus becomes hard.

Spatial distribution and resource allocation

Even inside one house, resources cluster. Private bathrooms are attached to certain rooms, and those rooms likely cost more, so comfort and access are partly bought through rent. Six rooms on the ground floor share one restroom, while three rooms upstairs have their own. At the campus scale, students who live farther from RPI, often because housing is cheaper there, pay with time and physical effort every day. Distance is a form of inaccessibility that does not appear on any building's checklist, but it decides who can get to class, office hours, and events when their body changes.

If I could redesign it

I would start small: adjust or replace the door closer so the door opens with less force, add a visible house number at the front, and replace the plywood with a real railing. At the neighborhood scale I would add street lighting along the route to campus and look at whether a shuttle or safe-ride option reaches houses this far out.

Three items: what they do and a design response

1. The threshold: front door and missing address

What it does. The heavy door with an overhead closer and keypad keeps the house secure and keeps heat in. The handwritten sign turns that into a rule for people: shut it tight. The missing address means the house only identifies itself at the back, to the mail carrier.

Design response. Adjust the closer to a lower opening force or add a push-button opener. Put a lit house number at the front, next to the existing motion light so they share power. Replace the sign with a latch that closes on its own, so security no longer depends on everyone pulling hard.

2. Vertical circulation: stairs, rails, restrooms

What it does. Three staircases connect three levels with no step-free alternative. The handrails make the stairs usable for a body like mine right now. Private bathrooms belong to specific rooms and everyone else walks a hallway to a shared one, so where you sleep decides how far a toilet is.

Design response. Replace the plywood with a proper railing, repair the treads, and add contrasting edges on each step. Make the chair at the top of the stairs an intentional rest point. In the lease, let residents request a room near a bathroom or on the ground floor when they are injured or disabled, even temporarily.

3. The route: house to campus at night

What it does. The 15-minute walk and the dark street decide when I can reach campus and whether I go alone. The motion light helps for the last few feet and leaves the street dark.

Design response. The counter-map above, drawn in fear and effort instead of distance. As a proposal: pedestrian lighting along the route, and connecting off-campus houses to a campus shuttle or safe-ride stop.

Written response

In HW2 I mapped my house after a back injury made the walk to campus too painful. For HW3 I wanted to map differently. A standard floor plan shows walls, doors, and stairs as fixed objects. It does not show that the front door is the hardest part of my day, that the stairs are fine because of the handrails, or that the street outside is scary at night. So my modified map measures effort, pain, light, and fear instead of only distance. This follows the idea in Counter Mapping, where Zuni artists make maps that show what the land means to the people who live on it instead of the borders drawn by someone else. My map is much smaller in scale, but it uses the same move: the person using the space decides what gets mapped.

Infrastructure beyond the objects

The door, the stairs, and the dark street were all shaped by things that are not physical objects. The biggest one is law. The ADA's public accommodation rules do not cover private homes, so a house like mine has no requirement to be accessible. The Fair Housing Act requires accessible design features in new multifamily buildings, but only for buildings first occupied after March 1991. Older houses like this one are exempt. Under the same law a tenant can ask for a "reasonable modification," but usually has to pay for it. The law treats access in old housing as a private problem for the disabled person to solve and fund.

Age and money also prevent change. Troy has a lot of old housing, and older houses get repaired with quick fixes, like the plywood on my stair railing. A landlord has little reason to adjust a door closer or add a ramp when the next tenant is probably a young, non-disabled student. That expectation becomes a loop: the house is built for a certain body, so people with other bodies don't move in, so there is no pressure to change it.

The campus scale matters too. Many RPI students, especially upperclassmen, live off campus in older houses in Troy, and rent tends to be cheaper the farther out you go. Distance and building age end up tied to money. A student who cannot afford housing close to campus pays in time and physical effort, and if they are disabled or injured, they may lose access to campus altogether. This can push future students away from RPI. A prospective student who uses a wheelchair, or who has chronic pain, has to ask not only whether classrooms are accessible but whether they can afford a place to live that they can actually get into and out of.

Stories told about these spaces

Aimi Hamraie, in the introduction to Building Access, argues that the design of space carries a story about who is expected to be there. Hamraie calls this the "normate template," the assumed average body that buildings are designed around. My house tells that story clearly. The handwritten sign assumes everyone can pull the door hard. The stairs assume everyone can climb. The address on the back door assumes the only stranger who needs to find the house is the mail carrier. The story of off-campus student housing is that it is temporary and that the people in it are young and healthy, so accessibility is not needed. My injury made me step outside that story for a few weeks, and the house suddenly felt designed for someone else.

What these infrastructures enable and disable

The infrastructures in my house enable independence for some people and take it away from others. The handrails let me keep living upstairs. The private bathroom in my room let me rest without climbing stairs at night. The heavy door kept me from coming and going easily, and I started planning trips to avoid opening it more than necessary. The dark street affected me emotionally as well as physically: I felt fear, and fear changed my behavior, so I stayed home at night instead of going to campus. Over time this shapes knowledge and belonging too. When I could not join the group workshop, I lost the shared knowledge my classmates built together. Access is not only about whether a body fits through a door. It also decides who gets to be part of a community, a class, or a campus.

Mapping my home showed me that inaccessibility is often made of small things that add up: a door closer, a missing address, a dark block, a rent price. None of them looks like a barrier on its own, which is why they rarely get fixed. A map that shows force, fear, and distance makes them visible, and that is the first step toward changing them.
